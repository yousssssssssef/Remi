# Offers lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

Scored on the business as described in the brief. The build is far ahead of the offer: there is a lot of product and no offer yet (no price, no named outcome, no guarantee, no channel).

## What is strong
- **Effort is attacked directly, which is where this category usually dies.** Receipt scanning into the kitchen in one step, cooking that deducts ingredients automatically, and "just bought" on the device all cut the logging work the buyer has to do. That is the right lever on the value equation.
- **A stack already exists.** Inventory with expiry dates, "use first", meal planner, shopping list, cook mode, nutrition dashboards, saved recipes from links, shared household kitchen. Each one answers a real step between "groceries in the house" and "dinner on the table without waste". It can be packaged; it does not need to be built.
- **A differentiator that is not price.** Arabic and English with 2,074 bilingual food aliases and 400 recipes is a specific advantage for MENA households. If it holds up against what is already sold there, the offer does not have to win by being cheaper.
- **The household, not the individual, is the buyer.** One subscription shared by several members raises the value per purchase.
- **The brand and mascot give the offer a name and personality** to build around, instead of a generic "pantry tracker".

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **The market may not be one where pain is massive or buyers are easy to reach.** Food waste, meal planning and nutrition tracking are real but mild, "nice to fix" problems for most households. Gulf households likely have spending power; North African ones may have much less, and these are very different markets lumped together. No channel is chosen, so "easy to find" is unproven. This lens passes most ideas on a weak market, and nothing in the brief yet shows this one is strong.
2. **Likelihood and time on the value equation are unproven.** No user has yet shown that households keep logging food after the first weeks (the brief lists retention as an unknown). Without that, there is no proof the outcome happens, and the payoff (less waste, money saved, better eating) shows up slowly. Low perceived likelihood plus a slow result is the most common way offers in this space fail.
3. **The device can break the offer economics.** A one-time hardware purchase with no unit cost, enclosure, certification or manufacturing plan, plus AI running costs per user that nobody has measured, could make the stack expensive to deliver. The stack rule keeps only what is cheap to deliver and valuable to the buyer; the device may fail the first half. It also raises the price of entry before any proof exists.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Which single buyer has the worst version of this pain and the money to fix it? (For example: a Gulf household of four-plus spending heavily on groceries, or a parent managing a family member's diet.) Pick one, not "MENA households".
- What is the dream outcome in one measurable sentence: money saved on groceries per month, kilos of food not thrown away, or meals planned with no effort? Which one do buyers care about most? (Answer with /founder-consumer.)
- What proof will make a stranger believe it works? Is there even one household that has used it for a month, and what changed for them?
- How fast does a new user get a first win: minutes (first receipt scanned, "use first" list shown) or weeks?
- What would a guarantee look like that the business can afford, for example "save at least X on groceries in 60 days or your money back"? X is unknown until the CFO models it (/founder-cfo).
- What do buyers already pay for this today, and what do the alternatives (other pantry and meal-planning apps, notes and WhatsApp lists, just cooking as usual) cost and do badly? (Answer with /founder-competitors.)
- What is the subscription price, the device price and the device unit cost? Could the price be doubled and still feel like the obvious choice? (Open: /founder-consumer, /founder-pricing, /founder-cfo.)
- Is the device part of the core offer, or a bonus or premium tier for later?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Narrow the market to one segment** in one geography (one country, one household type) with evidence that the pain is felt strongly and the money exists. Run /founder-consumer on that segment before anything else.
2. **Name one measurable outcome and prove it** with a small set of real households using the app for at least 30 days, showing they keep logging and get the result. This is the likelihood proof the offer currently lacks.
3. **Lead with the app, not the device.** Sell the subscription first; treat the device as a premium tier or bonus only after /founder-cfo shows a unit cost and AI cost per user the price can carry.
4. **Build the offer before setting the price:** run /founder-offer to stack the existing features against the buyer's obstacles, add an affordable guarantee and real (not fake) urgency such as a founding-household price, and give it a clear name. Then price it with /founder-pricing against what /founder-competitors finds, not by being cheaper.
