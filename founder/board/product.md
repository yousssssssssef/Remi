# Product lens
Score: 5/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **The craft is real.** 441 app tests and 157 backend tests pass, App Store readiness work (account deletion, privacy policy, terms) is done, and there is a bilingual catalogue: 851 foods with 2,074 English/Arabic aliases and 400 recipes, each with a photo. That is care in the parts nobody sees, and it usually predicts care in the parts people do see.
- **Arabic-first is a real experience advantage, not just a translation.** Matching receipts and typed foods through Arabic aliases is the kind of detail that makes the product feel built for this buyer rather than localised for them.
- **The loop closes on paper.** A receipt goes into the kitchen, the kitchen feeds the meal plan, cooking deducts the ingredients, and what is missing goes on the shopping list. When it works, that loop is the product.
- **The brand has a point of view.** The "Sip Happy" identity and the Remi mascot give it a face. Most kitchen tools in this space look like spreadsheets.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Logging fatigue breaks the loop.** Every feature downstream (use-first, meal plan, shopping list, nutrition, "Ask Remi") is only as good as the kitchen inventory, and the inventory depends on the household recording what they buy, use and throw out, every week. The brief itself lists "whether households will keep logging food over time" as unknown. If a household skips a week, the data drifts, the suggestions go wrong, and they stop trusting it. That is the most dangerous seam in the whole product, and nothing in the brief shows it has been tested with real households.
2. **No one thing.** The pitch takes a paragraph: inventory, expiry, receipts, recipe import, meal planner, shopping list, cook mode, nutrition dashboards with streaks and awards, widget, households, an AI assistant, and a hardware device. That is five products. A buyer in the first five minutes cannot tell which problem it solves for them, and a student team cannot make all five brilliant. The breadth is a sign the product is not clear yet.
3. **The device splits the experience before the app has earned it.** It is a second product with its own failure points (pairing code, Wi-Fi, offline cache, sync with the phone, hardware returns) and no enclosure, manufacturing, certification or unit-cost work done. Every hand-off between device, phone and backend is a seam where the experience can break, and the brief gives no evidence yet that it does something the phone cannot.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- In one sentence, what is the one thing Remi does that a household would miss if it disappeared tomorrow?
- Walk me through the first five minutes after download. When does the user get their first useful moment, and how much do they have to type or scan before it?
- Have real households used it for four weeks or more? What share of them were still logging in week four? (Unknown today; /founder-consumer can simulate it, but only a real pilot answers it.)
- If you had to cut half the features this month, which half goes?
- What does the device do that a phone on the counter cannot? Would anyone pay for it on its own? (Device price, unit cost and willingness to pay are unknown: /founder-cfo for unit cost, /founder-consumer and later /founder-pricing for willingness to pay.)
- Who are the households already solving this with a paper list, WhatsApp group or another app, and what do they complain about? (/founder-competitors)
- What does "Ask Remi" cost to run per active household per month? (Unknown: /founder-cfo.)
- Where does a hand-off fail today: receipt match errors, a wrong expiry guess, a household member who forgets to tick "used it up"? What happens to the user when it does?

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **Name the one thing and cut to it.** One sentence the buyer understands without a paragraph, and a launch build where every remaining feature serves that sentence. Nutrition awards, streaks and recipe import are candidates to hold back.
2. **Prove the loop survives real life.** A pilot with real households in the target market, with a measured share still keeping their kitchen up to date after four weeks, and evidence the first useful moment comes within the first session.
3. **Make logging nearly effortless before adding anything else.** Receipt scanning and "cooking deducts ingredients" are the right direction; the condition is that keeping the kitchen accurate takes seconds a week, not minutes a day.
4. **Park the device until the app retains.** Keep it as a prototype; no hardware spending, manufacturing or pre-sales until the app alone shows households stay, and until /founder-cfo has a unit cost and the panel shows people would pay for it.
